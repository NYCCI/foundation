**NYCCI**

**National Youth Coordination and Continuity Initiative**

**Foundation Document**

Nigeria · 2026

Prepared by

**Richard Lawal**

Chief Architect, NYCCI

**1.  The Problem**

Every year, young people across Nigeria organise projects, lead teams, serve communities, investigate problems, build initiatives, volunteer, and work with institutions.

The work itself may be valuable. What often remains after it ends is much less certain.

A student organisation changes its leadership and the incoming team has little record of what the previous team attempted. A group of young people completes a community project and the next group begins without knowing what was learned. A volunteer completes a year of service and moves on, taking with them relationships, experience, and knowledge that others may later need. An institution that once worked with capable young people may have no reliable way to know who contributed, what they did, or what was achieved.

As participants graduate, leaders change, projects close, and responsibilities move between people, useful knowledge can become scattered across individuals, conversations, documents, devices, and informal records. Previous work becomes difficult to find. Demonstrated contribution becomes difficult to establish. Activities that could have continued are sometimes started again from the beginning.

This affects both young people and the institutions around them. Young people may have substantial experience without a durable record of the work through which they gained it. Institutions may have recurring needs without access to the people, knowledge, and experience generated through previous efforts. Communities may repeatedly solve similar problems without being able to build effectively on what earlier groups discovered.

The result is a cycle in which valuable coordination occurs, produces useful experience and knowledge, and then much of that value becomes difficult to inherit.

NYCCI exists to address this problem.

**2.  The Idea**

The National Youth Coordination and Continuity Initiative (NYCCI) is a national initiative for organising meaningful youth participation with institutions, communities, and organisations, while ensuring that useful experience and knowledge generated through that participation can remain available for future use.

NYCCI works through programmes established around real activities and circumstances within the environments where young people already study, serve, work, organise, and contribute. A programme may bring young people together to address an institutional problem, support a recurring service environment, transfer responsibility between successive groups, or recover knowledge that has become fragmented over time.

Through these activities, participants contribute actual work and institutions receive actual results. The experience, evidence, relationships, and knowledge generated through that work can then remain available beyond the immediate activity and participants. NYCCI therefore provides a framework through which youth participation can contribute to work that continues to have value after the people who began it have moved on.

**3.  What NYCCI Does**

NYCCI operates through four initial programme families. Each exists for a distinct purpose, although a programme may produce results associated with more than one.

**3.1  YC²**

YC² operates through an established youth service environment, beginning with the National Youth Service Corps (NYSC) and its Community Development Service (CDS) structure. A group of Corps Members may identify a need within the community where they serve, organise themselves around it, carry out a defined activity, and leave a record of the work for those who follow. The programme provides a recurring setting in which successive groups can contribute, learn from previous activity, and continue useful work.

**3.2  Institutional Challenge Programme**

The Institutional Challenge Programme enables an institution to place a defined problem before a group of young people for investigation, development, or resolution. For example, a university may have a recurring difficulty with how students access an existing service. Rather than simply asking students for opinions, the institution can present the problem to a selected team, which investigates the situation, works with relevant people, develops a response, and presents its findings or solution. The institution receives the resulting work, while the participants develop and demonstrate experience through the work they actually performed.

**3.3  Continuity Programme**

A Continuity Programme supports the transfer of useful knowledge and responsibility when people, leadership, or teams change. For example, when the executives of a student organisation are replaced at the end of an academic session, the outgoing team may document its previous projects, unfinished work, important relationships, decisions, and lessons. The incoming team can use that record to understand what has already happened and determine what should continue. The programme helps an existing activity or institution carry its experience across a transition.

**3.4  Recovery Programme**

A Recovery Programme addresses knowledge that has already become fragmented, difficult to access, or at risk of being lost. For example, a community may have run an important youth initiative several years earlier, but its records may now be spread across former participants, old documents, photographs, messages, and institutional files. A recovery activity can bring those sources together, establish what can be reliably recovered, and organise the resulting record for future use. The programme makes previously inaccessible experience available again.

**The common thread**

These programmes operate in different circumstances, but each begins with real people doing real work within a real environment. What participants contribute, what institutions provide, what the activity produces, and what is learned can become useful beyond the immediate programme. The particular programme determines how that work is organised and what should be carried forward.

**4.  What Participation Produces**

NYCCI treats participation as more than being present at an activity. A person may join a team, but what matters is what they contribute once they are there.

A participant may organise people, investigate a problem, build a technical solution, conduct research, manage an activity, document a project, work with an institution, or take responsibility for a part of the work. These contributions create something that can be observed and recorded.

The progression is straightforward:

**Activity  →  Contribution  →  Evidence  →  Demonstrated Experience**

A participant who coordinates a community project can have a record of the people they coordinated, the work completed, the decisions made, and the result achieved. A participant who develops a technical component can have evidence of what they built and how it was used. Someone who conducts research generates a record of the investigation, the findings, and how those findings shaped the work.

Demonstrated experience comes from the work a person actually performs and the evidence that establishes that contribution. Over time, participation across different activities can therefore build a more useful record of a person's experience — one grounded in actual work rather than simply in attendance or claims.

**5.  From Participation to Opportunity**

A useful record of contribution becomes valuable when someone needs to find a person with relevant experience.

Consider a young person who has spent several years participating in NYCCI activities. During that time, they may have coordinated a community project, contributed technical work to an institutional challenge, conducted research, or worked with several different teams and institutions. If an organisation later needs someone with that kind of experience, the record of those contributions can help make that experience visible.

This creates a simple pathway:

**Participation  →  Contribution  →  Evidence  →  Visibility  →  Opportunity**

An opportunity may come from a university, government institution, organisation, community, programme partner, or another participant in the wider NYCCI network. It may take the form of employment, an internship, project work, leadership responsibility, mentorship, further collaboration, or another relevant opportunity.

For example, an institution that has worked with a young person through an NYCCI programme may later need someone who has already demonstrated the ability to coordinate a team and deliver a community project. The person's previous contribution can provide evidence relevant to that decision.

The result is a different relationship between youth participation and opportunity: work done today can remain evidence of what a person is capable of doing tomorrow.

**6.  How NYCCI Works Across Nigeria**

NYCCI is designed as a national framework that can operate within the different environments where young people already live, study, serve, organise, and work. The national framework establishes the common principles and requirements of NYCCI programmes. States provide the local structure through which programmes can be established and coordinated. Institutions, organisations, communities, and other participating environments provide the circumstances in which activities take place. Participants then carry out the work.

The relationship can be understood simply:

**National framework  →  State programmes  →  Institutions and communities  →  Participants  →  Activity**

A programme established in Ondo does not have to look exactly like one established in Kwara. The institutions involved, the activities undertaken, and the needs being addressed may be different. For example, a state with several active universities and innovation hubs may build programmes around those environments, while another state may work more extensively through community organisations, service groups, or other established youth networks. What remains consistent is the purpose and framework of the programme. This allows NYCCI to grow nationally without requiring every state or institution to operate in exactly the same way.

**7.  The Long-Term Possibility**

Imagine that a student organisation changes leadership every year, but the incoming executives can immediately understand the projects, decisions, relationships, and unfinished work of the team before them.

Imagine that a young person can look back years later and show not only where they participated, but the teams they worked with, the problems they helped solve, the work they produced, and the responsibilities they successfully carried.

Imagine an institution that has worked with hundreds of young people no longer having to rely entirely on memory or personal connections to know who has demonstrated useful experience.

Imagine a community building on a project completed several years earlier, because the record of that work survived the departure of the people who started it.

These are the possibilities that emerge when useful experience is allowed to remain available. NYCCI is built around a simple idea: coordination should not have to start from zero every time the people involved change. When activities are connected to the experience that produced them, when contributions remain evidenced, and when useful knowledge can be carried forward, individual activities can become part of something larger. Over time, the result can be a growing body of experience across people, institutions, communities, and states — one where experience can be inherited, contribution can remain visible, and institutions can build on what they have already learned.

**Technology**

As this body of activity grows, appropriate digital infrastructure becomes necessary to keep records connected, accessible, and useful. Where a programme requires persistent coordination, contribution records, relationships, evidence, or continuity across time, BuzzFeh may provide the digital infrastructure through which those requirements are supported. BuzzFeh is being developed as a coordination infrastructure platform. The technology serves the work.

**The direction**

NYCCI is working toward a Nigeria in which meaningful youth participation does not disappear when an activity ends or when the people who began it move on. Where experience can be inherited. Where contribution can remain visible. Where institutions can build on what they have already learned.

The goal is not simply to create more youth activity. It is to make the activity Nigeria already produces more useful, more connected, and more capable of carrying its value forward.
